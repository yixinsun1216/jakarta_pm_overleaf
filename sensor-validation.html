Sensor Validation

Indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) is measured by a Cubic PM2008M sensor inside the indoor device, while outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) is measured by Plantower PMS5003 in the Airly network sensors. One concern is that low-cost optical sensors may differ in calibration, and their response could vary with humidity, temperature and particle characteristics. We use two collocation exercises to quantify how these differences affect our results.

First, an Airly PMS5003 sensor was collocated with the US Embassy regulatory-grade monitor in Jakarta between May and July 2024 (Fig. S9a). We estimated an hourly \(R^2 = 0.90\), a slope of \(1.06\) and an intercept of \(2.9\). While the two sensors track each other closely, the Airly PMS5003 sensor reads on average 13% higher than the US Embassy sensor.

In the second collocation exercise, three indoor devices with Cubic PM2008M sensors were deployed side-by-side with an Airly PMS5003 outdoor sensor for two weeks in September 2026 (Fig. S9b–c; \(N = 349\) hours). The estimated hourly \(R^2\) values ranged from \(0.88\) to \(0.90\). Although the indoor devices closely tracked the outdoor sensor, they reported concentrations approximately 10 \(\mu g/m^3\) lower on average. The discrepancy is approximately additive, with device-specific slopes of 0.93, 1.06 and 0.88 (mean 0.96) and intercepts of \(-8.8\), \(-11.7\) and \(-5.6\) \(\mu g/m^3\). The offset is similar across the observed ranges of outdoor PM concentrations (23–100 \(\mu g/m^3\)), relative humidity (49–94%), and temperature (24–33\(^\circ\)C).

The offsets observed in the two collocation exercises likely arose for different reasons. In the first exercise, the positive offset of the outdoor PMS5003 sensor relative to the US Embassy monitor is consistent with documented overestimation by uncorrected Plantower sensors in tropical, humid settings (Hossain et al. 2025; Wulandari et al. 2024). In the second exercise, we attribute the negative offset of the indoor sensors to their placement inside the device, whereas the outdoor sensor was directly exposed to ambient air.

We invert the two relationships to map the recorded series into reference-grade-monitor units: \(O^{ref} = (O - 2.9)/1.06\) and \(I^{ref} = \big((I - a)/b - 2.9\big)/1.06\) and re-estimate our main results on the converted series. This exercise offers suggestive evidence that our headline results are robust and that our concentration estimates are, if anything, conservative. The re-estimated headline results are reported below.

Infiltration factor. The infiltration factor changes little: 0.62 (95% CI [0.45, 0.79]) against 0.59 in the main estimate (Fig. 2a, Sensor Adjusted). The conversion amounts to dividing indoor readings by \(b = 0.96\), since the intercepts are absorbed by the fixed effects and the outdoor sensor’s calibration rescales both sides of Eq. (1) equally. Because measurement error in the outdoor sensor may attenuate the calibration slope, we also estimate the relationship with outdoor readings as the outcome and indoor readings as the predictor. Inverting the resulting slopes yields values of 1.0–1.2 and implies an adjusted infiltration factor of 0.50–0.59. The sensor-adjusted infiltration factor therefore lies between 0.50 and 0.62, in all cases well above the US estimate of 0.29 (Lunderberg et al. 2023).

Concentration levels. The estimated offset implies that recorded indoor levels are conservative. Assuming that the offset also holds below the collocated range would imply that mean indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) is about 47.5 rather than 42.3 \(\mu g/m^3\), the ratio of mean indoor to mean outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) increases to 1.37 rather than 1.07 and the share of households exceeding the guideline on at least one day rises from 97% to 99%. The exact figures depend on how the outdoor sensor’s overestimation at low concentrations is modeled.

Source contributions. Source effects in \(\mu g/m^3\) (for example, the smoking effect) and comparisons across households (for example, the income gradient) are differences between indoor readings, in which a constant offset cancels. The source shares are affected because correcting the offset increases the estimated mean indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentration, while leaving source-specific differences largely unchanged. Recomputing these shares using the adjusted readings, the outdoor share falls from 56% to about 45% and the smoking share from 19% to about 17%, with the remainder attributed to the baseline (intercept) component. The ranking of sources is unchanged, and ambient outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) remains by far the largest contributor.
