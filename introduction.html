Introduction

Fine particulate matter (PM\(_{2.5}\)) poses the largest environmental risk factor to human health globally (Institute 2024). In 2018, among the 7.3 billion people directly exposed to unsafe average annual PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations globally, 80% lived in low- and middle-income countries (Rentschler and Leonova 2023). Rapidly growing cities face especially elevated levels of ambient air pollution, fueled by intensive use of fossil fuels, industrialization, construction and congestion (Kim et al. 2025; Landrigan et al. 2018). This pollution burden is expected to intensify: by 2050, an estimated 68% of the global population will reside in cities, with nearly 90% of this growth concentrated in Asia and Africa.

While epidemiological studies and policy evaluations typically rely on ambient PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations as proxies for human exposure, urban dwellers in high-income countries spend approximately 85–90% of their time indoors (Klepeis et al. 2001) and likely similar shares in urban LMIC settings. Because indoor concentrations can diverge substantially from ambient outdoor levels depending on building characteristics, ventilation practices, and indoor emission sources, ambient-based exposure assessment may be systematically biased (Avery et al. 2010; Evangelopoulos et al. 2020). Moreover, if the relationship between ambient and indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) varies systematically across households—particularly by socioeconomic status, ambient-based exposure assessment may also obscure important dimensions of environmental inequality.

Even though 3.45 billion people in low- and middle-income countries currently live in urban areas (World Bank 2023), we know surprisingly little about their indoor air pollution exposure. While solid cooking fuels remain a major driver of household air pollution where they are used — as emphasized in Global Burden of Disease models (Bennitt et al. 2025) — they tell only part of the story in LMIC cities, where clean cooking fuel adoption has accelerated and severe ambient pollution increasingly shapes indoor concentrations. A small but growing literature documents high infiltration factors in tropical LMIC cities (e.g., (Saha et al. 2025; Gurley et al. 2013; Anand and Phuleria 2022; Hossain et al. 2021; Kajjoba et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2011; Kephart et al. 2020; Fandiño-Del-Rio et al. 2020)), but individual studies typically cover fewer than 50 homes or short observation windows, limiting their ability to identify drivers of heterogeneity. This knowledge gap is fundamentally a data gap. Indoor air pollution data is very scarce, as very few households own indoor monitors, and those that do are generally wealthier and unrepresentative of the broader population.

We address this critical shortfall by collecting real-time measurements of both indoor and outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) in Jakarta, Indonesia—the world’s largest city (United Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division 2025), with over 40 million residents facing severe and persistent air pollution, sustained by traffic, industry, and residential combustion. We equip 283 urban households with indoor air quality monitors, capturing over 152,000 monitor-hours of indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) data. Importantly, these monitors did not display PM\(_{2.5}\) levels, so the monitors themselves did not induce differential behaviors. We complement these with outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) readings from 63 low-cost sensors, dramatically improving spatial granularity compared to the two reference-grade monitors previously available. Detailed household surveys further document demographic, behavioral, and housing characteristics, making this one of the largest studies on indoor air pollution concentrations among urban residents in low- and middle-income countries.

Using this rich dataset, we (1) document indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) levels and their relationship to outdoor concentrations, including extreme levels not captured by ambient monitors; (2) estimate infiltration factors, how they depend on housing characteristics and ventilation infrastructure, and compare them to existing LMIC and high-income country estimates; (3) decompose the sources of indoor PM\(_{2.5}\), distinguishing outdoor infiltration from hyperlocal sources such as smoking and waste burning; and (4) examine how indoor concentrations vary across income levels, despite similar outdoor pollution. Our household surveys provide essential context for interpreting these measurements. Together, these analyses allow us to assess whether ambient PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations adequately capture indoor concentrations in a rapidly urbanizing LMIC megacity, and whether the relationship between ambient and indoor pollution differs systematically across socioeconomic groups.
