\begin{abstract}
Exposure to fine particulate matter ($PM_{2.5}$) poses major health risks, especially in rapidly urbanizing cities. As urbanization accelerates, people in low and middle-income countries spend more time indoors, where pollution risks remain poorly understood. We present evidence from over 152,000 monitor-hours of indoor $PM_{2.5}$ measurements across homes in Jakarta, Indonesia—one of the world’s largest and most polluted cities. We find that mean daily indoor and outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ levels are both dangerously high, eight times above World Health Organization (WHO)'s health-based guidelines. In addition, indoor  $PM_{2.5}$ frequently reach hazardous levels—40 to 100 times the WHO guideline—levels that outdoor monitors do not capture. Unlike in developed settings, most indoor pollution originates from outdoor infiltration. Survey data also reveal large inequalities: lower-income households experience double the mean indoor $PM_{2.5}$ of higher-income households. Our findings show that indoor air pollution remains both severe and unequally distributed, even in this population where most people have adopted cleaner cooking fuels. Researchers and policymakers should integrate outdoor air quality mapping with demographically representative indoor monitoring to close key data gaps, enabling more accurate exposure estimates and better-targeted environmental health policies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% MOTIVATION
% Air pollution is a big deal
Fine particulate matter ($PM_{2.5}$) is the leading environmental risk factor to human health in the world \citep{health_effects_institute_state_2024}. 
% Developing countries
In 2018, among the 7.3 billion people directly exposed to unsafe average annual $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations globally, 80 percent live in low- and middle-income countries \citep{rentschler_global_2023}. 
% Cities
Rapidly growing cities face especially hazardous levels of ambient air pollution, fueled by intensive use of fossil fuels, industrialization, construction and congestion \citep{manisalidis_environmental_2020}. This pollution burden is expected to intensify: by 2050, an estimated 68\% of the global population will reside in cities, with nearly 90\% of this growth concentrated in Asia and Africa. 
% Relationship between exposure and ambient air pollution

Even though 3.45 billion people in low- and middle-income countries currently live in urban areas \citep{noauthor_urban_2023}, we know surprisingly little about their patterns of air pollution exposure. Most existing studies focus on rural households, where indoor pollution is primarily driven by the use of solid fuels for cooking. For instance, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) models household air pollution based on the proportion of the population using solid fuels for cooking \citep{bennitt_global_2025}. This approach underscores a broader knowledge gap: a limited understanding of indoor pollution sources and exposures in rapidly urbanizing environments. 

This knowledge gap is fundamentally a data gap. Outdoor air pollution data is limited, and indoor air quality data is even scarcer, as very few households own indoor monitors, and those that do are typically wealthier and unrepresentative of the broader population. We address this critical shortfall by collecting real-time measurements of both indoor and outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ in Jakarta, Indonesia—one of the world’s largest and most polluted megacities, where traffic, industry, and dense residential activity sustain persistently unhealthy air. We equip 308 urban households with indoor air quality monitors, capturing over 152,000 monitor-hours of indoor $PM_{2.5}$ data. Importantly, these monitors did not display $PM_{2.5}$ levels, so the monitors themselves did not induce differential behaviors. We complement these with outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ readings from 63 low-cost sensors, dramatically improving spatial granularity compared to the two reference-grade monitors previously available. Detailed household surveys further document demographic, behavioral, and housing characteristics. To our knowledge, this is one of the largest studies of indoor air pollution among urban residents in low- and middle-income countries. 

Using this rich dataset, we (1) characterize the relationship between indoor and outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations; (2) estimate infiltration rates of outdoor pollution into homes; (3) quantify the contribution of hyperlocal sources such as smoking and neighborhood waste burning; and (4) examine how indoor exposure varies across income levels. Our household surveys provide essential context for interpreting these measurements. First, respondents—primarily women—report spending approximately 80\% of their time at home, and their school-aged children spend about 70\% of their time indoors, making the home environment a critical exposure setting. Second, 98\% of households report using liquefied petroleum gas (LPG)  for cooking, reflecting substantial progress toward Sustainable Development Goal targets for clean cooking access. This context allows us to examine the persistence of indoor pollution even after a successful clean cooking fuel transition.

\section{Results}

\subsection{High Indoor $PM_{2.5}$ Levels}
%\subsection{Households are Exposed to High Indoor $PM_{2.5}$ with Episodic Spikes}

Our main finding is that average indoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations closely map outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ ambient concentrations on average, but episodic spikes of indoor $PM_{2.5}$ are often much higher than those of outdoor $PM_{2.5}$. Overall, this implies that households are on average exposed to higher levels of $PM_{2.5}$ indoor than outdoor in Jakarta.

% Similar high levels of indoor / outdoor
We first establish that both  indoor and outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ levels are at unhealthy levels. During our study period, the mean indoor daily $PM_{2.5}$ concentration was 40.7 $\mu g/m^3$, while the mean outdoor daily $PM_{2.5}$ concentration was 37.8 $\mu g/m^3$. Both levels were about eight times higher than the WHO annual standard of 5 $\mu g/m^3$, as shown on Figure \ref{fig:desc_pm}.

% In addition indoor has spikes
In addition to persistently high pollution levels, we find that indoor spaces also experience severe episodic pollution events. Figure \ref{fig:desc_pm}d displays hourly indoor pollution levels at the individual household level for a representative two-week period from our sample. The figure shows frequent spikes in indoor pollution, soaring far beyond levels characterized as hazardous by the US EPA and surpassing thresholds typically associated with emergency conditions. On average, indoor air exceeds US EPA's ''Very Unhealthy" threshold 4\% of the time--levels that are rarely observed in the outdoor measurements. 

% These indoor spikes matter to the extent people spend time indoor
In our accompanying survey, we find that respondents—90\% of whom were women—reported spending 80\% of their time at home. We focused on parents of elementary school children, who reported that their children spent an average of 70\% of their time indoors. Thus indoor pollution levels are highly representative of overall $PM_{2.5}$ exposure for women and children in our study population. Importantly, the sample targets low- and middle-income households, offering rare insights into a population largely overlooked in existing research on indoor air quality. Existing research on indoor pollution predominantly examines households in high-income countries \citep{krebs_using_2021, lunderberg_assessing_2023, burke_exposures_2022} or relies on small samples observed over short periods of time \citep{lim_comparing_2022}, leaving significant gaps in our understanding of indoor air pollution exposure in developing urban settings. More details on our sampling approach are in Section \ref{ssec:mehtods_sampling}.


Overall, these findings show that while outdoor levels capture average indoor exposure, they fail to account for the pollution burden from episodic indoor spikes. Individuals with similar average exposures may experience very different intensities and frequencies of severe pollution events—patterns that are missed when relying solely on outdoor pollution data. The health impacts of repeated short-term spikes remain poorly understood, underscoring the need for research that considers pollution spikes, not just mean levels.

\subsection{Outdoor Infiltration into Indoor Spaces}
\label{ssec:infiltration}

We model how ambient outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ infiltrates into indoor spaces; this decomposes the contributions of ambient outdoor sources versus hyperlocal sources to indoor pollution. Ambient outdoor refers to background $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations measured by our citywide sensor network, while hyperlocal sources—including smoking and neighborhood burning—occur at spatial scales too fine for our outdoor sensors to capture.

We estimate that a 1 $\mu g /m^{3}$ increase in $PM_{2.5}$ is associated with a .689 (95\% CI, 0.508, 0.871; $P < 0.001$) $\mu g /m^{3}$ increase in indoor $PM_{2.5}$ in the same hour. This rate is about 2.5 times the rate typically estimated in the United-States \citep{lunderberg_assessing_2023}, implying that in low- and middle-income countries, indoor spaces do not offer the same protection from outdoor pollution than in high-income countries. In addition, we find that infiltration happens much more quickly than in high income settings. We estimate that all infiltration happens within the hour, as opposed to estimated lagged effects up to 6 hours in high-income settings \citep{burke_exposures_2022, lunderberg_assessing_2023}. These estimates are robust to alternative regression models, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:infiltration}a. 

With these high infiltration rates, most indoor air pollution in Jakarta originates from the outdoors—unlike in high-income countries, where indoor pollution typically stems from indoor sources \citep{metcalfe_making_2025}. We estimate that 63\% of mean indoor PM2.5 comes from ambient outdoor pollution (Figure \ref{fig:infiltration}b).

To shed light on the causes behind these high infiltration rates, we examine heterogeneity in infiltration rates in Figure \ref{fig:infiltration}c. We find statistically significant differences based on air conditioning (AC) ownership (p = 0.08) and whether the monitor room has an open versus closed window or door (p = 0.03). These findings corroborate ventilation as a key determinant of infiltration rates. In Jakarta’s hot and humid climate, households without AC often rely on natural ventilation, increasing their exposure to outdoor pollution. 

Our results suggest that differences in infiltration rates are driven by long-term household characteristics. This is in contrast to previous studies in high-income settings that find that infiltration rates are primarily driven by short-term behavioral adjustments \citep{burke_exposures_2022, baquie_know_2024}. We use three analyses to show that  households are not actively taking short-term actions to reduce infiltration.

First, previous literature have found that infiltration rates decline at high outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations, suggesting that people actively modify their behavior to limit exposure at higher levels of outdoor pollution \citep{burke_exposures_2022,lunderberg_assessing_2023}. However, we do not find this relationship here. In fact, infiltration increases at higher $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations. This pattern, shown in Figure \ref{fig:infiltration}d, could be driven by several factors, including concentration gradients between outdoor and indoor pollution, meteorological conditions correlated with outdoor pollution levels, or the presence of high-emission hyperlocal pollution sources \citep{lunderberg_assessing_2023,macneill_factors_2012}.

Second, we find that households who believe outdoor air quality is unhealthy have analogous infiltration rates to those who believe outdoor air quality is relatively healthy. Households were classified based on whether they generally thought outdoor air pollution was unhealthy (high AQI vs. low AQI). As shown in Figure \ref{fig:infiltration}d, infiltration rates do not differ based on this belief, suggesting that differential beliefs are not translating to differential protective actions.  

Finally, we assess whether ventilation behaviors (e.g., opening windows or doors) respond to outdoor pollution levels. If households actively manage infiltration, we would expect them to close windows when pollution is high. However, Figure \ref{fig:infiltration}d shows no relationship between window or door openness and outdoor pollution levels. While opening or closing windows is a short-term behavior that can, in theory, be adapted, it is difficult to expect households to keep windows closed in a hot and humid climate like Jakarta, where natural ventilation is necessary for cooling. 




\subsection{Hyperlocal Sources of Indoor $PM_{2.5}$ Spikes}
While ambient outdoor pollution is the primary driver of persistently high indoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations, hyperlocal sources are responsible for the episodic spikes that lead to extreme pollution levels. Our survey, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:hyperlocal_sources}, clearly documents the impact of two major sources: indoor smoking and neighborhood waste burning.\footnote{It is worth noting that another major contributor to indoor air pollution that has been documented extensively is the combustion of solid fuels and kerosene for cooking. However, in Indonesia, the widespread adoption of LPG cookstoves has largely eliminated variation in cooking fuel use \citep{astuti_successful_2019}; in our survey, 98\% of households reported using LPG cookstoves for cooking. While LPG cookstoves still generate large spikes in pollution, our data do not include detailed information on cooking activities, preventing us from drawing insights on this source of indoor pollution.}

We find that households who reported indoor smoking within the last 24 hours experienced significantly higher indoor pollution levels. These households had a 5.51\% (95\% CI: 1.85–9.17, $P < 0.001$) greater probability of pollution spikes reaching very unhealthy levels. Given that the sample average probability of pollution spikes is 4.43\%, this translates to a 127\% increase. Analogously, smoking was associated with 20.8 $\mu g/m^3$ (95\% CI: 7.36–34.3, $P < 0.001$) higher average hourly indoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations, a 50\% increase compared to the sample mean.

Neighborhood-level waste burning is another significant source of indoor air pollution in Jakarta. Households in areas with more than two waste-burning events per week saw notably higher indoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations. However, accurately quantifying the effect of burning events on indoor pollution is challenging, as we rely on self-reported data and some events may be misreported. These measurement limitations contribute to wide confidence intervals around our estimated effects.

In addition to smoking and neighborhood waste burning, there are likely other hyperlocal pollution sources contributing to the observed spikes in indoor air pollution. Such activities may include cooking, traffic emissions, industrial operations, and commercial activities. An exhaustive list of all polluting activities is beyond the scope of our analysis.

\section{Discussion}

We show that indoor $PM_{2.5}$ exposure in Jakarta is both persistently high and unequally distributed across income groups. In addition to elevated mean levels, indoor environments frequently experience hazardous pollution spikes—extremes not captured by outdoor monitors. These spikes occur despite 98\% of households in our sample using liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) cookstoves, which are typically considered clean cooking. This suggests that addressing indoor air pollution in low- and middle-income countries requires a broader framework than is currently used.
For example, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework defines household air pollution in terms of solid fuel use—a metric that has guided policy and resource allocation globally. However, this definition may not fully capture the indoor pollution risks experienced by urban populations using cleaner fuels but still exposed to high ambient and infiltrated pollution. 

We find that most indoor pollution stems from outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ infiltration, with an infiltration factor (0.689) substantially higher than estimates in high-income countries. These high rates are related to structural characteristics, such as the absence of air conditioning and the dependence on natural ventilation, rather than short-term behavioral responses. While information campaigns may increase awareness of infiltration risks, economic and infrastructural constraints likely limit behavioral adjustments. Mitigation efforts must therefore prioritize  pollution controls at the source, targeting major contributors like vehicular traffic and industrial emissions. In the interim, coupling outdoor air quality mapping with demographically representative indoor measurements can help better characterize true exposure and inform more equitable policy design.


In contrast, episodic peaks in indoor pollution are driven by hyperlocal sources such as indoor smoking and neighborhood waste burning—activities more amenable to individual or local intervention. Information campaigns and stronger enforcement against garbage burning could reduce these extreme events and their associated health risks.


Our findings also have important distributional implications. Consistent with previous studies \citep{greenstone_indoor_2021, burke_exposures_2022, ito_willingness_2020}, we show that low-income households are exposed to the highest levels of indoor air pollution. Relying solely on outdoor air quality as a proxy for exposure may systematically underestimate both overall exposure and disparities across income groups. These inequalities stem from both higher infiltration rates—associated with poorer housing conditions—and greater hyperlocal emissions. 


Finally, while our study provides novel insights into indoor $PM_{2.5}$ exposure in a developing urban setting, it has several limitations. First, our sample focuses on  households with elementary school-aged children, and therefore is not fully representative of Jakarta’s population. Future research should assess whether similar patterns hold for other demographic groups, particularly groups with heightened health risks, such as the elderly or those with pre-existing health conditions. Second, while we identify key determinants of indoor air quality, we do not capture all possible hyperlocal pollution sources, such as cooking emissions, incense burning, or traffic-related infiltration, nor are we able to track personal exposure outside the home. Relatedly, our reliance on stationary indoor monitors limits our ability to account for variations in individual exposure as people move between environments. Expanding real-time monitoring to include wearable sensors and more detailed measurements of indoor pollution sources could provide a fuller picture of exposure risks.

\section{Methods}
High levels of outdoor air pollution are sustained in Jakarta throughout the whole year, and historically especially high between the months of May and October. We design the study to cover this high pollution season and conduct it in two successive rounds, with approximately 154 households participating in each round. Round 1 was conducted from 1 June to 28 August 2024. Round 2 was conducted from 10 September to 10 December 2024. This randomized controlled trial was registered on the AEA RCT registry (Identification No. AEARCTR-0013110) and received IRB approval from the University of Chicago (Protocol No. IRB23-0310) and from Bandan Riset Dan Inovasi Nasional (Application No. 29012024000032).
% DATA
Individual respondents consented to the collection of the indoor air quality data and survey data used in this study. Ambient outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ data comes from devices monitored by our hardware implementation partner, not associated with private individuals, and therefore without personally identifying information.


\subsection{Study Participants and Datasets}
\label{ssec:mehtods_sampling}

\paragraph{Study Participants:} Households participating in our study were selected to cover households in Jakarta. We selected our sample by partnering with the Education Office in Jakarta, which compiled a representative list of Jakarta schools. We randomly sampled households from school rosters and successfully enrolled 308 participants, 285 of whom successfully installed the hardware, connected to Wi-Fi, and operationalized the devices for data collection. Therefore, all our participant households have at least one elementary school child. Surveying challenges like low participation and high attrition are common in developing country cities \citep{greenstone_indoor_2021}. Our sample strategy was designed to address these challenges and ensure the proper use and return of air quality hardware.

Figure \ref{fig:desc_pm}a presents the geographic distribution of participating households and demonstrates the wide coverage across the city. Households in our sample are relatively larger and less wealthy than the average Jakarta household but covers a wide range of socio-economic backgrounds. For more information on how our sample compares to the wider Jakarta population, see Appendix Table \ref{tab:covariates}.

Following enrollment, the survey enumerators visited households to obtain informed consent and administer a baseline survey. This survey captured household demographic information, housing structure characteristics, and beliefs and behaviors related to indoor and outdoor air pollution. Upon consent, air quality hardware was installed in the home for approximately three months, during which it collected continuous indoor air quality data.

\paragraph{Indoor $PM_{2.5}$:} The air quality hardware was composed of a fan equipped with an internal $PM_{2.5}$ sensor and was installed in each participating household. The sensors were designed to collect $PM_{2.5}$ data in real time, with readings not visible to respondents. When connected to Wi-Fi, the devices transmitted data to a remote server at five-minute intervals, which were subsequently aggregated to the hourly level for analysis. The hardware was produced by Nafas, a Jakarta-based air quality technology company. 

To incentivize households to keep the hardware on, participants received IDR 35,000 (approximately USD 2) every 30 days, contingent on the hardware being connected to Wi-Fi and operational for an average of 14 hours per day. For households without Wi-Fi or unwilling to use their Wi-Fi for the experiment, dedicated Wi-Fi units were installed. To offset electricity costs, participants received an additional IDR 15,000 (approximately USD 1) every 30 days.

\paragraph{Outdoor $PM_{2.5}$:} Our hardware partner, Nafas, maintains over 100 low-cost outdoor sensors across the city. For each household, we calculate a weighted average of air quality readings from the three nearest outdoor sensors, assigning weights inversely proportional to the distance to each sensor. The 63 outdoor sensors used in this study also capture temperature and humidity data, which we use as controls. The outdoor low-cost sensors were installed prior to our study and were not randomly distributed across space, which could introduce non-randomly distributed measurement error. We test for selection in the location of outdoor sensors by regressing a household's distance to the closest outdoor sensor on recorded indoor $PM_{2.5}$, recorded outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ of this sensor, household income, house size, whether the household owns an AC, household head employment status, and household head education level. We do not find evidence of selection bias on observables, as shown in Appendix Table \ref{tab:sensor_selection}.

\paragraph{Natural ventilation behaviors:} To complement our main survey efforts, we deployed a short WhatsApp survey to participating households on three separate occasions during the study period. The survey asked respondents whether the windows and doors in the room housing the indoor monitor had been open or closed during the past hour.


\subsection{Infiltration Rate}
The average indoor infiltration rate is defined as the increase in indoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentration per unit increase in local outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentration. To estimate this rate, we used a regression model at the monitor-hour level. For each household $i$ in hour $t$, the infiltration rate ($\beta$) was estimated using the following specification:
\iffalse
\begin{align}
\label{eq:infiltration}
    PM^{In}_{it} &= \alpha + \beta PM^{Out}_{it} + \pi T_{it} + \gamma H_{it} + f(i, h, w) + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{align}
\fi
\begin{align}
\label{eq:infiltration}
    PM^{In}_{it} &= \alpha + \beta PM^{Out}_{it} + \pi T_{it} + \gamma H_{it} + \mu_h + \mu_w + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{align}
where $PM^{In}_{it}$ and $PM^{Out}_{it}$ denote indoor and outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations, respectively. $T_{it}$ and $H_{it}$ are controls for contemporaneous outdoor temperature and humidity. Hour-of-day controls $\mu_h$ capture diurnal patterns in pollution and household activities, and week-of-sample controls $\mu_w$ capture temporal trends and broader atmospheric conditions.


Figure \ref{fig:infiltration}a presents the results of this regression model for this main specification and alternative ones: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Main specification: includes controls for average variation in $PM_{2.5}$ over the course of the day (hour-of-day fixed effects, $\mu_h$) and average weekly trends (week-of-sample fixed effects, $\mu_w$).
    \item Specification 1 and adds controls for time-invariant differences across households ($\mu_i$).
    \item Specification 1 and adds controls for household-specific average variation in $PM_{2.5}$ at different times of the day (household-hour-of-day fixed effects, $\mu_{ih}$).
    \item Specification 1 but restricts the sample to households located within 1 km of a low-cost outdoor sensor to assess the potential impact of measurement error in outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ concentrations on infiltration estimates.
\end{enumerate}

The estimated infiltration rate ($\beta$) remains highly stable across all specifications. We chose specification 1, which includes hour-of-day and week-of-sample fixed effects, as our main specification to enable us to examine heterogeneity by household-level characteristics, which would not be possible in the presence of household fixed-effects ($\mu_i$). 
\\ 

We further investigate lagged effects of outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ with the following specification:
\begin{align}
     PM^{In}_{it} &= \alpha + \sum_{k = 0}^{11}\beta_k PM^{Out}_{it} + \pi T_{it} + \gamma H_{it} +\mu_h + \mu_w + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{align}
We present the results when including 11 lags (outdoor $PM_{2.5}$ at each of the previous 11 hours) and ensure that the results are robust to the inclusion of additional lags. From this regression, we derive an estimate of outdoor-indoor infiltration by calculating the cumulative effect of a 1 $\mu g/m^3$ increase in outdoor concentration on indoor concentration:
\begin{align}
    \beta' = \sum_{k=0}^11 \beta_k
\end{align}
Infiltration rates derived from this lagged regression are very similar to our main estimates, given that most of the infiltration happens within the first hour, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:inf_lag}.
\\

To examine heterogeneity in infiltration rates across household characteristics $X_i$, we run the following interacted specification:
\begin{gather}
    PM^{In}_{it} = \alpha + \beta_1 PM^{Out}_{it} + \beta_2 X_i + \beta_3(PM^{Out}_{it} \times X_i) + \pi T_{it} + \gamma H_{it} + \mu_h + \mu_w + \varepsilon_{it} \notag
\end{gather}
