Envelope Tightening: Back-of-the-envelope

Following (Chen and Zhao 2011), in steady-state, indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations \(C_{in}\) can be expressed as a function of outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations \(C_{out}\), the air exchange rate due to infiltration a, the penetration factor P, the particle deposition rate k and the indoor-source strength by volume S: \[\begin{aligned} C_{in} &= \frac{a P C_{out} +S}{a+k}. \end{aligned}\] The coefficient \(\beta\) estimated in Eq. (1) can be shown to correspond to the infiltration factor \(F_{inf} = \frac{a P}{a + k}\). For the central back-of-the-envelope calculation, we set \(P = 1\) and \(k = 0.5 h^{-1}\) and solve for the implied air exchange rate \[\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{\hat{F}_{inf} k}{P - \hat{F}_{inf}} \end{aligned}\] Given \((a, P, k)\), we recover indoor-source strength by volume as the residual \[\begin{aligned} S &= (a + k) C_{in} - a P C_{out}. \end{aligned}\] Robust range: we vary the deposition loss rate \(k\) over \([0.2, 1.5]~(h^{-1})\) and the penetration factor \(P\) over \([0.8, 1.0]\). We evaluate the four corner combinations \((k_{min}, P_{min}), (k_{min}, P_{max}), (k_{max}, P_{min}), (k_{max}, P_{max})\); for each we solve for the implied \(a=\frac{\hat F_{inf}k}{P-\hat F_{inf}}\) and recompute \(S=(C_{in}-\hat F_{inf}C_{out})(a+k)\).
This model can be leveraged in a simple back-of-the-envelope calculation to assess the impact of interventions that reduce air exchange (targeting a) or reduce particle penetration of incoming air (targeting P) on indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations. This latter calculation isolates the effect of changing P alone, holding a fixed. In practice, physical mechanisms that reduce P may also reduce a. Table S4 reports the key inputs and main results of this calculation, split by income and smoking status.
The back-of-the-envelope exercise suggests that reducing particle penetration (lowering \(P\)) mechanically decreases indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) in all households—under the convention that \(P\) applies only to outdoor-to-indoor particle entry—whereas weatherization (reducing \(a\)) can have ambiguous effects because it also limits dilution of indoor emissions. In our central calibration, weatherization increases indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) in low-income households, and this conclusion is robust over the parameter range considered. In contrast, weatherization decreases concentrations in high-income non-smoking households throughout the robust range; for high-income smoking households, the effect remains small and can change sign over the robust range. Precisely quantifying the impacts of interventions that alter \(a\) and \(P\) would require stronger empirical identification of these parameters, which is beyond the scope of this paper.
