Beyond Stoves and Outdoor Monitors: Indoor PM2.5 Concentrations and Inequality in a Fast Growing Megacity

  • Yixin Sun
  • Jeanne Sorin
  • Daniel Suryadarma

Abstract

Exposure to fine particulate matter (PM\(_{2.5}\)) is the leading environmental risk factor for mortality worldwide. Epidemiological and global health research typically proxies exposure using cooking fuel type and ambient outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations. Yet urbanization is changing the pollution profile of low- and middle-income country (LMIC) populations who use cleaner fuels and spend most of their time indoors, but live in areas with high outdoor pollution - raising questions about whether either proxy captures actual exposure. We present 152,000 hours of indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) monitoring from 283 homes in Jakarta, the world’s largest and one of the most polluted cities, where 98% of households have adopted clean cooking fuels. Clean cooking fuels deliver large reductions in indoor PM2.5 relative to biomass-dominated settings, yet indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) is persistently high. Mean indoor PM2.5 was 42.3 \(\mu g/m^3\) (eight times the WHO annual guideline), and 97% of monitored households did not meet the WHO 24-hour guideline. We estimate an infiltration factor 2-3 times larger than in high-income countries and find that the largest share of indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) originates outdoors. We uncover stark inequality in indoor concentrations: low-income households experience 112% higher indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) than high-income households despite similar outdoor concentrations. This inequality is mostly driven by differences in indoor emission sources, particularly smoking. Low-cost indoor monitoring reveals what neither cooking fuel type nor outdoor concentrations can capture: actual indoor concentrations and their stark distributional consequences in rapidly urbanizing megacities.
