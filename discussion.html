2 Discussion

We show that indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations in the world’s most populated city are persistently high, where 97% of monitored households did not meet the WHO 24-hour guideline, which requires the 99th percentile of daily mean PM\(_{2.5}\) to be at or below 15 \(\mu g/m^3\). While high indoor levels are primarily driven by infiltration from ambient outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\), outdoor concentrations are an imperfect proxy for exposure. Indoor and hyperlocal sources contribute significantly to pollution levels and induce extreme short-term pollution events not captured by outdoor monitors. Despite comparable ambient outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) levels, households in the lowest income quartile experience more than double the indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentration of those in the highest quartile. This disparity is largely driven by smoking and other indoor sources, with additional evidence suggesting that differences in housing characteristics might also play a role. Neither cooking fuel type nor outdoor concentrations, the proxies commonly used in global health frameworks and epidemiological studies, fully capture these patterns.

We estimate an infiltration factor of 0.59 in our main specification (0.50-0.78 across specifications and sensor calibrations), substantially higher than estimates in high-income countries. This estimate is broadly consistent with, and extends, a growing body of evidence from urban LMIC settings such as Dhaka (Saha et al. 2025), Mumbai (Anand and Phuleria 2022) and Hong Kong (Hossain et al. 2021). This evidence has, to date, been largely constrained by small sample sizes (typically less than 50) and short monitoring windows (typically 24 hours). With over 152,000 monitor-hours across 283 homes, together with detailed household surveys, our study offers substantially greater statistical power to characterize the distribution of indoor PM\(_{2.5}\), the dynamics of infiltration of outdoor air into indoor spaces, how infiltration depends on housing characteristics and ventilation infrastructure, the contribution of indoor and hyperlocal behaviors on average daily pollution and pollution spikes, as well as their determinants across a socioeconomically diverse urban population. Our estimates are significantly different from high-income settings (Krebs et al. 2021; Lunderberg et al. 2023; Burke et al. 2022), emphasizing the need for more research in LMIC cities.

This high infiltration factor is likely driven by both a high penetration factor and a high air exchange rate, reflecting the prevalence of natural ventilation and Jakarta’s hot and humid climate. Unlike in high-income settings where households reduce infiltration during high pollution episodes by closing windows and using air filtration (Burke et al. 2022), we find no evidence of such protective behavioral responses in Jakarta. This suggests that avoidance strategies effective in HICs may not transfer to LMIC contexts, where air conditioning is less prevalent and heat limits the ability to keep windows closed.

SI Appendix, Table S4 clarifies the mechanisms underlying this pattern using a one-compartment mass-balance model that distinguishes particle entry from ventilation-driven dilution. The framework shows that measures that primarily reduce natural ventilation, such as closing windows or tightening the building envelope, are not uniformly protective. Although reduced ventilation can lower the entry of outdoor pollution, it also limits the dilution of indoor emissions. As a result, in households with substantial indoor sources, particularly households with smokers, these actions can trap indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) and increase concentrations. To the extent that indoor sources can be reduced, this would not only lower exposure directly but also expand the scope for net-protective household actions, such as closing windows during outdoor pollution episodes, and for building retrofits. The contribution of these indoor sources also raises mismeasurement concerns when ambient outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) is used as a proxy for exposure even though indoor-sourced episodic peaks are not captured by outdoor monitors.

Our findings have important distributional implications. Consistent with previous studies (Greenstone, Lee, and Sahai 2021; Burke et al. 2022; Ito and Zhang 2020), we show that low-income households experience the highest indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations. The socioeconomic gradient in indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentration is not due to heterogeneous ambient outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) but rather to higher prevalence of indoor and hyperlocal sources among lower-income households. Accurate exposure assessment may therefore require coupling outdoor air quality mapping with demographically representative indoor measurements. This pattern is consistent with recent evidence from London showing that the income gradient in indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) can be steeper than the corresponding gradient in ambient outdoor pollution (Metcalfe and Roth 2025).

Importantly, our estimated mean indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) of \(42.3~\mu g/m^3\) — more than eight times the WHO annual guideline value of \(5~\mu g/m^3\) — is substantially lower than in biomass-dominated settings like Dhaka (\(\sim 190~\mu g/m^3\)) (Gurley et al. 2013), yet higher than LPG-using households in Accra (\(\sim 24~\mu g/m^3\)) (Zhou et al. 2011) and comparable to Lima (\(41.1~\mu g/m^3\)) (Kephart et al. 2020), where outdoor traffic-related pollution rather than cooking is the dominant driver. This pattern, corroborated by recent evidence from Dhaka showing indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) nearly equivalent to outdoor levels even in households transitioning away from solid fuels (Saha et al. 2025), underscores that ambient outdoor pollution, rather than fuel type alone, can be a binding constraint on indoor air quality in densely polluted LMIC cities. Taken together, these studies consistently document substantial indoor PM\(_{2.5}\) concentrations in urban LMIC settings; our contribution is to measure these concentrations at scale over longer periods and to quantify heterogeneity, spikes, and inequality within a single megacity.

This highlights the need for a broader framework for understanding indoor air pollution in these contexts. For example, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework defines household air pollution in terms of solid fuel use—a metric that has guided policy and resource allocation globally. However, this definition does not fully capture the indoor pollution risks experienced by urban populations that use cleaner fuels but remain exposed to high ambient and infiltrated pollution, as well as additional indoor or hyperlocal sources like smoking or waste burning.

Despite these contributions, our study has several limitations. First, our sample focuses on households with elementary school-aged children, and therefore is not fully representative of Jakarta’s population. Future research should assess whether similar patterns hold for other demographic groups, particularly groups with heightened health risks, such as the elderly or those with pre-existing health conditions. Second, indoor and outdoor PM\(_{2.5}\) were measured using different low-cost sensor models. A two-week collocation of three of the study’s indoor devices with an outdoor sensor, conducted after the study in outdoor air (SI Appendix), suggests that the monitors closely track each other over time (hourly \(R^2\) between 0.88 and 0.90) but that indoor devices record concentrations about 10 \(\mu g/m^3\) lower than the outdoor sensor. Under the assumption that this offset applies to in-home conditions during the study, the infiltration factor is robust (0.50–0.62 depending on the calibration) and our estimates of indoor concentration levels are conservative. A longer collocation of all devices against a reference-grade monitor would allow future work to correct indoor levels directly.

Third, while we identify key determinants of indoor air quality, we do not capture all possible hyperlocal pollution sources, such as cooking emissions, incense burning, or traffic-related infiltration, nor are we able to track personal exposure outside the home. Additionally, although we do not estimate different infiltration factors across income groups, we observe differences in housing characteristics such as air conditioning and ventilation infrastructure. Observable differences in housing quality may not map one-for-one into reduced-form differences in infiltration because these features affect both the entry of outdoor pollution and the dilution of indoor emissions. Larger samples and more direct measures of airflow and housing characteristics could help clarify these mechanisms. Relatedly, our reliance on stationary indoor monitors limits our ability to account for variations in individual exposure as people move between environments. Expanding real-time monitoring to include wearable sensors and more detailed measurements of indoor pollution sources could provide a fuller picture of exposure risks.
